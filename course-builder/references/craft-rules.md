# Craft rules: voice, handholding, format

These are the rules that make a course read like the user made it, and read like it was
made for a beginner. They are the difference between a course people finish and one they
bounce off.

## Voice: the knowledge is the source's, the voice is the user's

The biggest, most repeated failure is writing in the source author's voice. When you
model a course on a strong source (a Hormozi book, an ACQ handbook, a famous curriculum),
you unconsciously copy its cadence: the clipped maxims, the drill-sergeant imperatives,
the set-apart one-liners for gravitas, the chest-beating. Strip all of it.

- **Find the user's voice profile and treat it as law.** For RIOA/Vik it lives at
  `~/.claude/skills/copywriting-master/references/vik-voice-default.md` (the old OperatorBrain profile is lost). Read it fully. If
  no profile exists, ask the user for 2-3 samples of how they actually talk/write and
  build a quick one.
- **Match an approved sample in-document.** Once one section is approved, later drafting
  agents should be told to sit next to it seamlessly.
- **Strip the source's tics** (unless the user's voice happens to share them): clean
  parallel maxims ("Winners go hard. Winners don't quit."), press-release symmetry,
  gravitas one-liners on their own line, drill-sergeant barks, top-1%/stadium
  chest-beating, rhetorical "and I know it's true, because...".
- **Write in the user's actual register.** For a casual founder: talking to a mate across
  a table, contractions, connective ellipses, "look/honestly/here's the thing," cuss where
  it genuinely lands, analogies from ordinary life. Care through bluntness.

### Running a voice-conversion pass (when a draft drifted)
Spawn agents against the voice profile + an approved anchor sample. Rule: preserve every
fact, number, framework, checklist, and any quoted lines that are in a DIFFERENT
deliberate register (e.g. a buyer-facing script stays in the buyer's register, not the
author's voice). Change only the teaching prose. Give the agent explicit before/after
examples of the tics to kill.

## Zero assumed knowledge, no blindspots

The reader has never done the thing and never read the sources. Hold that in your head on
every line.
- **Explain or rename every insider term the first time it appears.** Don't drop "breathe
  the script" or "isolate the objection" cold; say what it means in plain words right there,
  or rename it entirely.
- **Never gloss a heading with jargon** ("the four-phase gate, adapted"). Say what it is
  in plain English ("your first two weeks, day by day").
- **No source citations in the body.** No "(ACQ)", "(Hormozi)", "(corpus: X)". They make
  it a research paper and mean nothing to a beginner. Keep the sources map in a private
  brief.
- **Add explanation, don't assume it.** If a step needs a sentence of setup a beginner
  would trip without, add the sentence.

## Format

- **Short paragraphs.** Mostly 1-3 sentences, then a blank line. Break dense blocks at
  natural thought-boundaries. Walls of text are the enemy of a beginner.
- **The five-part module shape** every time (see `module-template.md`). Predictability is
  a feature; the reader always knows where they are.
- **Honor the voice's hard bans.** For Vik: no em-dashes anywhere (use ellipses/commas),
  no cliches, no guru-speak (game-changer, 10x, unlock, elevate), no corporate formality
  (furthermore, utilize, leverage-as-verb, corporate "we").
- **Thorough beats short.** Fill every gap; just say it simply. Length is not a problem.

## The worked example

Teach the transferable craft (works for anything), then show it landing in ONE concrete
running example the reader follows the whole course. In each module's "Watch it happen"
section, drop into that example. Reserve one late module for the example end-to-end, so
they see a complete working machine and how every module connects.
