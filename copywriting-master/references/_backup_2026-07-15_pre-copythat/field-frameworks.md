| **Risk-reversal ladder (weak→strong)** | Money-back-if-no-result → result/performance guarantee → "you don't pay until I deliver." Buyers fear *losing money* more than they want the win, so decrease the risk rather than inflate the promise. Field variants: "10× better than money back" + *proactive refund reminder* (Myron Golden); "if I can't save you ≥20% I won't even propose" (Koniak); guarantee only what you control; *micro risk-reversal* "send me your cold email, I'll send a revision — no cost, no ask; if you like it, let's talk" (Josh Braun); "remove the excuse — money-back guarantee" (30MPC); **bake the guarantee into the headline itself** — "Increase your store's conversions in 3 days or I'll refund your money" (Neville Medhora); **radical transparency as risk-reversal** — hand the buyer a written list of every reason *not* to buy / every skeleton in the closet up front; they'll find them in diligence anyway, so disclosing first builds trust (Suli Ali); **pay-on-results stated up FRONT as the door-opener** (not just a close) — "I'm so confident this'll work that if I don't get you clients, you don't pay a penny" — the guarantee is the cold-call/first-meeting hook (Jordan Platten); **performance-aligned offers carry it natively** — "$2k–$5k upfront + commission, guaranteeing five figures of incremental sales in the first 7 days," or commission-only when proof is thin (Liam Ottley). **Believability caveat (Joanna Wiebe):** a guarantee that reads *too good* can backfire — "Double your conversion rate or your money back" can trigger "wait, double? *really?*" skepticism, where a softer, more believable claim ("over a year, 9 in 10 of our clients more than double their conversion rates") sometimes out-pulls the bold guarantee. Tune the promise to the edge of believable, not past it. **Make the buyer's risk as close to zero as humanly possible** (Iman Gadzhi): with ~100%-margin digital products, run rev-share — "you only spend money once you make money, zero risk" — and "keep beginner risk as little as humanly possible so you can keep spinning the slot machine"; a buyer who risks nothing has no reason to say no. **Lead the cold outreach WITH the guarantee** (Ravi Abuvala): on cold traffic the risk-reversal *is* the hook — "you only pay if you have show-up appointments," "you only pay when you close a deal," "we guarantee 30 bookings / 5 clients a month" — and fused with offer-specificity ("we've helped 378 businesses just like you get the loan in 30 days or less, under 7%, no collateral") it lifts you above every competitor in the inbox. No case studies = a risk reversal is **mandatory**. **Lead WITH the guarantee, and make it emotional** (Brandon Bornancin): don't bury it — *position* on it ("$1M–$5M in sales in 12 months; if our clients don't sell at least $1M in a year, we're pissed off") so the conviction itself sells, and frame inaction as the risk-free-but-worthless option in a **two-choice close** ("two choices: do nothing, which is 100% risk-free… or invest this tiny amount for all this value"). | Any offer, especially cold traffic or thin proof. | **20** — Daniel Fazio, Patrick Dang, Joel Kaplan, Frank Kern, Rory Vaden, Stephen Pope, Jose Rosado, Matt Macnamara, Myron Golden, Trent Dressel, Ian Koniak, Grant Cardone, Josh Braun, 30MPC, Neville Medhora, Suli Ali, Jordan Platten, **Iman Gadzhi**, **Ravi Abuvala**, **Brandon Bornancin** |
| **Take-away / disqualification close** | Be the matchmaker, not the closer: "It's not for everybody," "I only take N clients," willing to walk. Field: "sell the test drive, not the car — never close on a cold call" (30MPC); "push the prospect away to get the truth — have you considered just hiring another clerk?" (30MPC); **strip-lining** — "Honestly, it doesn't seem like this is a problem for you" — they disqualify or push back and confirm the pain (Pete Kazanjy); **fall on your sword** when nothing lands — "sounds like I missed the mark, you've got it figured out — what's been working well?" (30MPC); **label the price-obsession** — "sounds like price is your number one priority" (they disqualify or surface what matters more than price); "walking the beach — sounds like you've got this buttoned up, take care" / "do me a favor and fire me after this call" (Josh Braun); "go for the NO early — is it worth walking away for a few thousand?" (Bryerton); the "unsell": "before price — do you even think this solves it *a bit* better than what you do now?" (KD). The enemy is "maybe," not "no." **UCP — Un-ideal Customer Profile** (Rex Galbraith): proactively tell a poor-fit prospect "we're not a good fit," and **be willing to turn revenue away** — overselling licenses they won't use creates ~50% shelfware that kills the renewal; knowing who you're NOT for is as important as who you are. **Walk-away raises perceived value** (Ashley Welch) — turning down more business than you bring on makes the right clients lean in; "we're not a good fit" is itself a close. **Detachment-as-leverage** (Karen Kelly): "if I can only do a *little* better than what you're doing now, we shouldn't be talking; if I'm *much* better I'll push hard — and if not, I'll be the first to tell you to get out of here." **Disqualify the eager-but-wrong-fit buyer** (a16z/Waseem Daher): be blunt even when they're ready to pay — "even though you're *willing* to buy, you shouldn't, because we won't serve you well" — and set a **price-floor disqualifier** ("we work best for companies spending at least $X/mo; below that you probably shouldn't pick us"). **Financial-qualifier as the filter** (Liam Ottley): "if they won't put up $1–2k for a proper foundation, they're not the client you want; if they don't blink at it, you know they're someone you can work with." **Set the "we're not for everyone" frame early + a budget-floor disqualifier** (Greg Hickman, *folded as enrichment, no count bump*): open with "if at any point we realize we can't help you or aren't the best fit, we'll do whatever we can to point you in the right direction" (lets you exit a bad-fit call gracefully later), and gate on price *before* the demo — "to work together our minimum engagement is X; assuming you love what you see, is that doable, or is that an issue?" ("I value my time more than a bad-fit client"). **Reject the slow buyer outright** (Andrew Argue): cut prospects who won't decide after one or two value calls — "if you won't decide after three meetings but expect *prospects* to decide after one, it won't work"; the best clients decide fast, pay up, and refer, so disqualifying the ditherer protects the model. **The advisor take-away + walk-away-as-leverage** (The Futur / Chris Do, *folded as enrichment, no count bump*): "I'm not sure you need this — I don't think it's the best use of your time, money, or effort" (genuine, not a tactic — it makes them say "wait, I *will* buy this from you"); "not everybody who can afford you is your customer," "if it's not a hell yes, it's a hell no," and for a low-baller, **disqualify the *budget*, not yourself** — "where else could that $1,000 make a bigger impact?" then refer them out. | When a prospect hesitates or over-eagerly agrees. (Canon: **Meta Close** / **Doctor Not Waiter**.) | **18** — Patrick Dang, Jose Rosado, Frank Kern, Rory Vaden, April Dunford, Matt Macnamara, Myron Golden, Grant Cardone, Ian Koniak, Trent Dressel, Josh Braun, 30MPC, Steven Bryerton, Pete Kazanjy, Rex Galbraith, Ashley Welch, Waseem Daher/a16z, **Andrew Argue** |
| **Sell the result, not the mechanism** | "Sell the vacation, not the flight." Lead with the outcome/experience; the *how* is nearly irrelevant. Field language: "nobody buys swords, they buy the superpower" / "you sell fireballs, not flowers — the flower is the feature, the fireball is what they can now do" (Josh Braun); "people buy business outcomes, not speeds & feeds" (Koniak); "sell the payoff not the pieces / the grass not the seed" (Myron Golden); "say what it *means*, not what it *is*" (30MPC, demo-the-why); the headline is the end-result + customer + timeframe, the mechanism never appears (Neville Medhora); **sell the *initiative*, not the feature** — tie the feature to a funded business priority all the way to revenue ("help sellers forecast better SO THAT revenue is predictable and investors gain confidence"); closer to revenue = more believable (30MPC/Kyle Coleman). **Outcome > benefit > feature ladder** — amateurs sell features (the logo/typography), the average sell benefits (a flexible asset bank), pros sell the *outcome* ("a seamless brand experience across every touchpoint"); always pitch the outcome — "you're not selling a pen, you're selling confidence, status, future success, transformation" (Brand Master Academy, Patrick Dang). **Lead with the outcome even when the mechanism is "AI"** — "if you lead with AI instead of an outcome, in 2026 you'll get crushed… AI is just the delivery mechanism; what people actually want is certainty and leverage — stable revenue, time saved, risk reduced" (Liam Ottley); category-reframe away from the product entirely — "I have never, ever heard a founder say 'I can't wait to buy accounting software' — they want it done, they don't care how it gets done" (a16z/Waseem Daher); on a cold call **never name the service** ("paid ads," never "Facebook ads" — naming the mechanism is a trigger that turns people off), and "sell them what they want, give them what they need on the call" (Jordan Platten). **Lead with WHO you help, not what you BUILT** — Microsoft moved from "a computer on every desk" (the object) to "empower every person and organization to achieve more" (the outcome); answer the reader's "what's in it for me?" and "why should I care?" *before* you name the thing (Joanna Wiebe). **Sell futures, not features** — "a benefit is just a better future the reader can picture"; the **3 Laws of selling**: (1) don't talk about your product/category, (2) who you work with matters more than what you do, (3) sell *futures* not features; always frame from the buyer's *life*, never the product — "Nobody is Selling Marketing Funnels" (sell the outcome the funnel produces, not the funnel itself) (Mike Killen). **The "email value premise" — people want the *result*, not more emails** (Pat Flynn, *folded as enrichment, no count bump*): nobody wants another email, they want the transformation it promises — so make the lead magnet deliver that transformation *faster*; "more content = *less* value, because the reader's time is scarce," shorter-and-quicker-to-result outsells longer-and-more-complete. **Homepage headline = say what it is / what you GET / what you can DO with it** (Flux Academy / Ran Segall): three fail-proof homepage formulas, all benefit-or-outcome-led — kill "Welcome to my website" / "SMS marketing is the future" and write "Text marketing for Shopify ecommerce" or "29,100 icons & illustrations, fully customizable"; specificity beats clever. And the two pillars of any persuasive email are *short* and *about THEM* — "here's how I can help *you* grow," never "please help me promote my thing." **Lead with the benefit, demote the feature** (DesignCourse / Gary Simon, *thin guest — folded as enrichment, no count*): "A developer you can trust, available 24/7/365" beats "JavaScript developer"; "I'll help you cultivate a bond between your product and your users" beats your name. | Every offer, headline, pitch. The #1 convergent field principle. | **19** — Hormozi, Daniel Fazio, Patrick Dang, R.W. | **Anchor high + trade every concession** | Quote above your floor; never drop price for free. **Four Levers of Negotiation (Todd Caponi):** every discount must be *paid for* with one of — (1) volume, (2) timing of cash / prepay, (3) length of commitment, (4) timing of the deal. "Trade never give" (Dressel); "anchor high then invite a counter — there's no cost to anchoring high" (30MPC); **concede on terms, not value** — teach the legitimate lanes to a discount (longer term, a committed sign-by date), and tie the discount to *signature speed* (15% if signed in 2 weeks, 10% in 4) so it drives velocity instead of eroding price (Firenzi); **anchor against the prize, not the competitor** — quantify their #1 initiative's value ($5M) first so a $50K gap feels trivial (30MPC); a *"light lock-in"* (pre-schedule the kickoff) worth ~10% of ACV beats discounting; **popcorn pricing** — price tiers non-linearly so the biggest is wildly the best value-per-unit (Belal Batrawy); make the discount hard to get (defer to a manager / CC the CEO, send 4 hrs later); anchor against premium competitors then be the value pick — "we charge $50K/week; McKinsey/Bain/BCG charge $200K/week — we're 75% cheaper but better, and we're actual practitioners" (Jesse Pujji). **Solution-first, then subtract** (Giuseppe Conti) — present the full right-fit solution; to reach a lower price, *pull scope out* ("we can get there, but it'd mean taking this away") so every concession costs them something; **discounting is permanent** — discount once and you discount every future deal too; **good/better/best three-option + a recommended pick** (buyers default to the middle, and laying out all three surfaces add-ons they didn't know existed); **anchoring rule** — if you *don't* know the market, let them go first; if you *do*, go first and lead with value identified ("4 projects that save you $20M"), not price (the "War Dogs" lesson: bidding first-and-low when you don't know the market can leave 10× on the table); **anti-commoditization** — vary the *mix* not the margin (swap a senior consultant for a junior, Fiat/Lexus/Rolls per need) to lower cost without giving away price. **Good/better two-tier with a baked-in bonus** (CharliMarieTV): a Standard tier anchored by an Extended one carrying a stack of extras ("there's *always* a bonus thing with the extended license") pulls buyers up — a font priced $12 Standard vs $26 Extended (commercial + web-font + matching vector pack "drawn with the same pen" + license certificate) sold 45 in 5 days, only **6 chose Standard**. The cheaper option exists to make the dearer one the obvious pick. **Three-tier 1X / 2X / 4–5X with abundance-coded tier names** (Pat Flynn): price a base (1X), a middle (2X with more bonuses), and a premium (4–5X with everything) to pull buyers to the top — e.g. **$37 / ~$74 / ~$147** — and *name* the tiers so the top one feels generous, not greedy ("Combo Meal," "All You Can Eat Buffet"); the decoy middle and the abundance-framed naming both push the buyer up the ladder. **Anchor by *relative scale*, and let *them* name the budget first when you don't know it** (The Futur / Chris Do): price every line item against the big number — "$60K on the logo but not $250K on the rollout? cheap out on production and it's lipstick on a pig" — because **whoever names the number first sets the frame**; **price-bracketing / "sonar"** floats a wide round range ("could be 10k, could be 50, could be 200") and reads the reaction to locate the budget; the budget-elicitation question "in what *ballpark* have you prepared to spend?" then mirror it back ("so you're prepared to spend $50–60k, is that right?"); and **pricing IS positioning** — with similar objects people assume the dearer one is better, so state the price plainly and say "**projects start at [X]**" to leave room to go *up*. **The value-stack anchor** (Brandon Bornancin): itemize every component with a dollar value, total it to a big number ("$27,717"), then collapse to a single small price ("just one payment of $970") and anchor against a real reference cost ("the website alone is $1,500/year — you get that free") so the ask feels tiny beside the stacked value. | Pricing & negotiation on any call/page. | **19** — Patrick Dang, Jacob Cass, Joel Kaplan, Frank Kern, Cole Gordon, Matt Macnamara, Trent Dressel, Ian Koniak, Myron Golden, 30MPC, Todd Caponi, Belal Batrawy, Jesse Pujji, Firenzi, Giuseppe Conti, **CharliMarieTV**, **Pat Flynn**, **The Futur (Chris Do)**, **Brandon Bornancin** |
| **Pain → Dream → Bridge (A→B / gap-island)** | Make painful Point A vivid, make dream Point B vivid (and numeric), position your offer as the only bridge; raise the stakes on the pain side so they jump. Field: "before & after — what sucked before they paid vs. what got better after; mine LinkedIn comments for the before, testimonial pages for the after" (Josh Braun); "existing state → promised land" (30MPC); **Heaven & Hell** — paint the vision *and* what happens if unsolved; the gap creates urgency (Alex Kremer). **Offer Messaging Stack (OMS)** — build the pitch on the customer's *burning need* (known or unknown), then use *provocative selling* — "punch" the burning need and speak straight to the challenge to provoke the buy (Jeremy Miller); **Latent Pain → Realized Pain → Desired Solution** — move them from vague discomfort to felt urgency *before* presenting any solution ("no pain, no sale") (Patrick Dang). **DOS — Desire → Obstacle → Solution** (Joanna Wiebe, *folded as enrichment*): open by matching their *desire*, name the *obstacle* in the way (the "enemy"), then introduce the *solution* + link — "the obstacle is the magic middle"; same A→B spine, just led from the dream side. **Point A → Bridge → Point B, stated cleanly** (Flux Academy / Ran Segall): a sales-call script (and the proposal after it) guides the client from current state (A) to desired state (B) with your offer as the *bridge* — and **map their exact words from the call back into the proposal** so they read their own A and B; the textbook articulation of the gap-island spine. | Discovery, leads, sales pages, demos — structural spine of a pitch. | **13** — Patrick Dang, Cole Gordon, Matt Macnamara (GAP), Joel Kaplan, Stephen Pope, Frank Kern, Ian Koniak, Trent Dressel, Josh Braun, 30MPC, Alex Kremer, Jeremy Miller, **Flux Academy** |
| **Make the prospect sell themselves** | Ask, don't pitch — they voice the pain and the solution. "Be interested, not interesting; hand them the mic" (Koniak, 30MPC); ask one question then *silence* — "the second thing they say is worth more than the first" (Dressel). **Make them hold the benefit (BLC, Kevin Dorsey):** instead of "does that make sense?", ask "how much faster is that than what you do now?" / "who'd you roll this out to first?" so the benefit comes out in THEIR words. 80/20: listen 80%, talk 20%. **The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick):** never ask "would you buy this"; confirm (a) they have the problem and (b) they're the archetype, then close "you said you wanted X, I'm offering X — do you want it?" (~80% close). **Diagnose by trying to close mid-discovery** — if they balk at the offer, the need was weak; the balk itself is the diagnostic (MFM). **Push-pull / permission to deprioritize** — give explicit permission to say it's NOT a priority so the honest answer surfaces; **no-lose confirmation** — "here's what I'm gathering, do I have this right?" (yes = you look sharp, no = they hand you the truth). **Socratic objection close (Hormozi):** never attack a third party — for "my spouse won't approve," walk a question ladder ("Why wouldn't he? He wants what's best for you, right? Does he know you're struggling? Would he be happier if you kept struggling? Then would you be opposed to moving forward today?") so the prospect argues your case for you; meet any objection with *childlike curiosity* ("Huh, that's interesting, tell me more"), then ask questions that lead them to their own conclusion. **Live diagnostic-model sell (Joanna Wiebe):** on the call, draw your framework live (e.g. a triangle — List / Offer / Copy) and have *them* mark each component red/yellow/green; they self-diagnose, see exactly what's broken, and think "I hope I can afford this person" — **self-identified problems build the most trust.** **Confirm-back close:** "You said you have problem A. Feature X addresses A — which is good for you, because solving A is one of the things you confirmed you wanted." **Walk them into selling themselves first** (Andrew Argue): before upselling an existing client, get a string of yeses on the value you already delivered — "we did a great job on your tax prep, didn't we?" → "yes" — so they reinforce the trust *out loud*, then segue to the next service; and **let the prospect talk 80%, you talk 20%** so the pain and the fit come out in *their* words, not yours. **The 1–10 trap — "why isn't your score *lower*?"** (The Futur / Chris Do, *folded as enrichment, no count bump*): get the prospect to rate their desire/readiness 1–10, then ask **"why isn't it lower?"** — to defend the number *they* list every reason your thing is worth buying, selling themselves to you; you can put *anything* on a 1–10 scale ("if you say it can't be measured, you're just not creative"). | Discovery & close on any call; content that asks the reader's own questions back. | **15** — Patrick Dang, Cole Gordon, Jose Rosado, Vanessa Lau, Joel Kaplan, Myron Golden, Ian Koniak, Brian Tracy, Trent Dressel, Josh Braun, Kevin Dorsey, Rob Fitzpatrick, Hormozi, Joanna Wiebe, **Andrew Argue** |
| **Problem-first discovery: dig & quantify the pain** | After permission, name 2–3 crispy 5th-grade problems your buyers face; ~90% one lands, else they correct you. **Problem Proposition (vs value prop):** spend ~80% on the problem, ~one sentence on the solution — Gong (300M+ calls): buzzword value-prop ≈5.5% success vs problem-led ≈16% (**3×**). **Problem/Discovery Tree:** Situation → Operational problem (champion's daily annoyance, 4–5-fig pain) → Executive problem (C-suite risk, 6–7-fig pain) — climb it to sell bigger. **Chunk up by seniority** (above/below the line): managers get the *process* problem, VP/C-suite get the *business* problem attached. Then ladder: "Why is that a problem? What's it costing you?" — a problem isn't a problem until you quantify it. **Invert your benefit/feature copy into pain** to *generate* the questions — flip each benefit line into the painful before-state it implies ("a more scalable way" → "so today it's taking tons of time / things are breaking"); **"So what?" laddering** pushes a small pain up to C-level/existential impact; **"typically" + pylon** language ("a lot of folks also deal with C and D") surfaces more pain and makes them feel understood. *Poke-the-Bear / illumination* (Josh Braun): find a problem, don't ask about one; **Magic-Moment** Q: "when did you realize that was a problem?" turns pain into a story. **Value-before-validation:** "nobody cares if your product works if they're not already convinced that it working matters" — validate "if this worked, does it matter to you?" *before* any demo (30MPC/Mallika Sahay). **Pattern-match on initiatives, not industry/persona:** find why customers actually bought (IPO path, M&A, intl expansion) and target prospects showing that same trigger (30MPC/Kyle Coleman). **Context beats content** (John Barrows) — "marketing is content, sales is context"; never ask "tell me about your priorities," wrap the question in research ("we work with CMOs in healthcare who say X, Y, Z — walk me through yours"); pre-flight every question with the **"why do you need to know that?" test** (assume they flip it back — no damn-good reason, cut it) and lead with *industry-level* questions so they talk about what they're the expert in (Basho ladder). **Three Layers Deep on the pain** (Jordan Platten): L1 expand the pain (what's broken + how it feels) → L2 opportunity cost / urgency (why now) → L3 "I need YOU to fix it" — "most salespeople stop at layer 1, but deals close at layer 3"; **doctor-not-pharmacist** (diagnose before you prescribe; summarize the gap back before solving — "you're at X, want Y, tried Z which caused this — right?"); extract **three admissions before pitching** — "this is a real problem," "it needs fixing now," "I can't fix it alone" (miss these and you get "let me think about it / send an email"). **The three buyer questions every deal must answer** (a16z): "Why do anything?" → "Why you (vs. competition, including do-nothing)?" → "Why now?"; **As-Is vs. To-Be** — articulate current state from discovery, then the future state with your solution. **Problem-matching before pitching** (Joanna Wiebe): prove you understand the prospect's *specific* problem in their *exact language* first, or the solution "just sounds like noise — stop pitching, start mirroring"; and **agitation = an interview you put yourself through** — keep asking what the problem really *costs* them (time, money, standing) until the pain is vivid and quantified. **"Go wide, go deep" + the bedside test** (Mike Killen / Troy Dean, *folded as enrichment*): list ALL their goals → pick the #1 → ask "why is that important?" repeatedly until you hit the true buying motive (sell the business / retire) — *that* motive is what you sell; and frame the real problem the way the buyer feels it — "picture your client in bed at night: what are they *actually* stressed about?" — in their language, not yours. **Surround the pain + "get them naked"** (Andrew Argue): when you hit a pain point, don't move on — *stab it from different angles*, "like a dentist pressing the sore tooth until they're in a panic to get it fixed"; and run a **rapid-fire stack of uncomfortable, specific questions** (how many bank accounts / credit cards, business + personal debt, what you pay yourself, what you set aside for taxes) — "the more uncomfortable, the better" — so the buyer *feels* the disorder before you prescribe anything. **Sales = change management: Current State → Obstacle → Desired Future State** (The Futur / Chris Do): the *only two questions* are "what do they want?" and "what's getting in their way?" — establish both before anything else; run the **Clarity Call** (control the frame → **identify the gap** between where they are and where they want to be, since "a bigger gap = more value" → test the time-frame "1–10, how important is fixing this in the next 30–60 days?" → guide the next step) and **qualify on buying *power*, not budget** ("budgets are set by finance for where they *are*" — ask about turnover, deal value, the capital unlocked if the problem is fixed). The whole posture is diagnostic — "**if you want to get rich, teach people about the problem and make them *problem-aware***." | Cold calls, problem-aware leads, demos, VSL pain-stacking. | **14** — Matt Macnamara, Patrick Dang, Trent Dressel, Myron Golden, Josh Braun, 30MPC, Kevin Dorsey, Keenan, Jason Bay, Kyle Coleman, Jordan Platten, Joanna Wiebe, Andrew Argue, **The Futur (Chris Do)** |
| **Cold-call / cold-DM opening formula** | **Tailored Permission Opener** (Gong-best, 11.18% vs 2.15% for "did I catch you at a bad time"): (1) lead with something about THEM (a trigger/research) — *context first is what breaks the telemarketer stigma*, (2) own that it's a cold call ("I'll be honest, this is a cold call — but a well-researched one"), (3) permission micro-ask ("can I steal 30 seconds on why I called, then you can hang up on me?"). Variant: **"heard the name tossed around"** (11.24%) — lead with a shared thread (investor/competitor/geo) before your name. Then value as OUTCOME not feature; interest-based CTA. **"The reason for my call is ___" is the most timeless opener** — "humans crave reasons" (Cialdini's "because" / Xerox-line study: *any* reason lifts compliance ~60%→~94%); if you can't finish that sentence, don't make the call (John Barrows, Chris Orlob). Build it from a case-study result: "the reason for my call is we showed [peer in your industry] how to drive [result] — worth a conversation?" Beats trendy openers ("How have you been?") that have short shelf lives. | Any cold outbound — call, DM, or email open. | **11** — Trent Dressel, Matt Macnamara, Patrick Dang, Rory Vaden, Ian Koniak, Josh Braun, 30MPC, Giulio Segantini, Armand Farrokh, **Chris Orlob, John Barrows** |
| **OPPS / 3x3 cold-email anatomy** | Observation/trigger (buyer-focused, never open with "I") → Problem tied to it + its impact → one-sentence Solution → **interest- or offer-based** CTA → PS. 50–100 words, 5th-grade English, one idea per email, white space, mobile eye-test, follow up 3–4×. Named field variants that all reduce to this: **REPLY method**, **3x3**, Josh Braun's **4-line** (emotion/true-situation → problem/stakes → 3rd-party credibility → low-friction ask) and **Mad Libs** fill-in system. **Personal > personalized:** a true situation the whole segment faces *scales* when 1:1 personalization isn't possible. ## ⚠️ Currency flags
- **NEW — the literal word "AI" now tanks cold-email reply rates (hard data, 2025–26).** Gong's 85M-cold-email study: **"AI" ≈ −33% to −36% reply rate**, buzzwords ≈ **−57%**, asking for time ≈ **−44%**; offer-based CTA ≈ **+28%**, personalized opener ≈ **5×**. The em-dash and over-polished phrasing read as an AI tell ("strip the dashes"). Sell the emotional outcome, not "AI-powered [feature]." *(30MPC/Gong, Josh Braun.)*
- **NEW — data-backed B2B buzzword blacklist ("SNS test" — Sounds Nice, Signifies Nothing):** "optimize," "streamline," "accelerate," "single source of truth," "single pane of glass," "platform," "world-class," "cutting-edge," "leading provider," "end-to-end," "scalable." Replace with "dumb human language" at a 4th-grade level. *(30MPC, Josh Braun.)*
- **AI = augmentation, not automation (2025–26) — strongly corroborated.** Winning move is AI+HI ("be the last mile / Iron Man + Jarvis"; "we won't be replaced by AI, we'll be replaced by reps who use AI" — Barrows). **New angle (Barrows) — human imperfection is now a deliberate trust signal:** a flubbed voicemail/video gets *more* callbacks than a polished one ("AI is perfect, we aren't — and that's our superpower"); and prospects who *sniff* AI pretending to be human disengage instantly ("it just doesn't have a soul"), but *flag it as AI up front* and they respect it. **Nuance (Patrick Dang, 2025–26):** the *results*-buyer is AI-agnostic — "people don't care if something is AI or not, they care about the results" — so the tension resolves cleanly: buyers don't reject AI *per se*, they reject AI-*detectable slop* and AI *pretending to be human*. Use AI freely, never let it degrade quality, and disclose it rather than disguise it. **Strongly reinforced this cycle (Liam Ottley):** "if you lead with AI instead of an outcome, in 2026 you'll get crushed" — owners have *tried* the AI tools and weren't impressed, so AI is the delivery mechanism, never the pitch (see *Sell-the-result*); "the difference between a demo and something production-ready is chalk and cheese" — sell *reliability*, not magic. Same human-imperfection note from Mike Locke's **reality-TV principle** — "imperfection + transparency builds trust; everyone has a guard up before they buy, and a raw founder video lowers it." Enterprise echo (a16z): "no one wants to run a brand campaign and have a hallucination that violates consumer trust" → sell human-in-the-loop ("if I can review every action before it goes out and it's faster, maybe I trust it *more* than the humans"). **The silent-AI objection in creative/service work (The Futur / Chris Do, RIOA-relevant):** the deadliest version is never *spoken* — the buyer quietly thinks *"can I just get a Midjourney output for $10 instead of paying you $10,000?"* and ghosts; "they're not going to tell you 'I didn't understand the proposal' — the unsaid is you losing the deal." So you must *pre-empt* it: sell the judgment / taste / outcome the $10 tool can't deliver, never the deliverable the tool can mimic. *(Frank Kern, Patrick Dang, Ian Koniak, John Barrows, 30MPC, Liam Ottley, Mike Locke, The Futur.)*
- **"Build it ourselves with AI" is the dominant B2B objection (2025–26):** ~73% of sellers hit it. Counter by quantifying who *owns/maintains/secures* the build + opportunity cost of engineering time.
- **"Cold calling is dead/sucks/that's why it works" is a fully saturated contrarian hook.** The corpus is now drowning in these titles — the *permission opener* and *reason-I'm-calling* tactics still work; the headline gimmick is tired.
- **CONFLICT to flag — "Is now a bad time?" (Voss, good) vs "Did I catch you at a bad time?"
