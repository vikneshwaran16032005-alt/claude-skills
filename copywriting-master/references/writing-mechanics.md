# Writing Mechanics

The tactical craft of putting words on a page. These are the techniques that separate copy that sounds like a human being from copy that sounds like a press release.

---

## Dimensionalization (Copy That)

Show, don't tell. Make abstract benefits visceral by adding sensory, temporal, or situational specifics.

### The technique
Take a generic benefit statement. Then recast it with specific dimensions — who, when, where, how it looks, how it feels, what exactly changes.

### Examples

❌ "You'll be confident in meetings."
✅ "On Monday morning at 9:15, when your CEO turns and says 'what do you think?' — you'll have the answer ready before the silence gets awkward."

❌ "Sleep better at night."
✅ "You'll fall asleep in the time it takes to cross the hall from the bathroom to the bed. No more 2 AM staring at the ceiling. No more checking the clock and calculating how much sleep you have left."

❌ "Save money on groceries."
✅ "Walk out of Whole Foods with two full bags for what you used to spend on one."

### Rule of thumb
If you can replace the benefit with any other product's benefit and it still works — it's not dimensionalized. The benefit must be SPECIFIC to this product's mechanism.

### Halbert's visceral version
Halbert's famous "picture words" technique: use concrete, pictureable nouns instead of abstractions. "A brand-new 2-story cabin on Jackson Lake" beats "a vacation property." "A white 1959 Corvette with red leather seats" beats "your dream car."

The reader's brain can't picture abstractions. It CAN picture concrete details. Every abstract benefit needs a concrete anchor.

## Writing Headlines (combining Ogilvy, Caples, Schwartz, Hopkins)

### The rules

1. **The headline's job is not to describe the product. It is to stop the right reader.** A headline that describes the product is often the worst-performing option (tested by Caples).
2. **Headlines pick the reader.** Same ad, different headlines, 5-10× variance in returns. The headline is the single highest-leverage sentence you'll write.
3. **A headline change on an existing ad can 5-10× returns.** If testing budget is tight, test headlines first.

### The process

1. Write 20 headlines using Schwartz's 38 verbalizations (see `lead-types.md`).
2. For each, ask: "Would my target reader stop scrolling / reading / walking past for this?"
3. Cut the top 3. Sleep on them if possible.
4. A/B test the top 2.

### High-leverage tactics

- **Specificity wins.** "Lose 17 pounds" > "lose weight". "$4,385/month" > "good income".
- **Numbers grab the eye.** Especially odd / specific numbers — "7 reasons" > "several reasons".
- **Questions engage if they match what the reader is ALREADY thinking.** "Tired of feeling tired?" works if they are. Fails if they aren't.
- **"You" and "your" pull the reader in.** Second-person direct address beats third-person every time.
- **Because** is the second most powerful word in copy (after "you"). Any reason, even a trivial one, dramatically increases compliance (Langer's Xerox study: 93% compliance with "because I'm in a rush" vs. 60% with no reason).
- **News triggers engagement.** "Announcing", "new", "finally", "just released" — use when honestly applicable.
- **Curiosity gaps.** State something surprising, then hold back the mechanism or resolution. Don't resolve it in the headline — make them read for it.

### Failure modes to avoid

- Cleverness for cleverness's sake. Puns, alliteration, poetry — usually cost clarity. Use only when they genuinely sharpen the hook.
- Describing the product instead of describing the outcome.
- Generic benefits ("the best", "top quality", "amazing results").
- Abstract claims with no concrete picture.

## Writing Bullets / Fascinations

Bullets are mini-headlines. Each must intrigue enough that the reader wants to know the answer. When done right, bullets are the single best sentence-level copywriting exercise you can practice.

### Structure of a great bullet

Each bullet teases knowledge (the "what") without revealing the specifics (the "how"). The reader has to buy / click / keep reading to get the answer.

**Bad:** "Learn what foods cause inflammation."
**Good:** "The one breakfast food — doctors eat it, dietitians recommend it — that triggers inflammation in 78% of people over 40. (Page 43)"

### Templates

- "The [X] that [surprising result]"
- "Why [common belief] is wrong — and what to do instead"
- "How to [benefit] without [common sacrifice]"
- "The [adjective] secret of [authority figure] — revealed on page [N]"
- "[Number] signs your [body/business/relationship] is [bad state]"
- "Before you [common action], check this [first]"
- "The [mistake] almost everyone makes when [activity]"

### Bullet density

In a long-form sales page, bullets are power-tools. A single section can have 8-15 bullets. The reader scans them, and each one that intrigues increases buy-intent.

Never write bullets as a summary of what's already stated. Write them as teasers for things NOT stated.

## Transitions and Eye-Relief

Reading copy on a screen is physically tiring. Your job is to pull the reader through effortlessly. Transitions and eye-relief are how you do it.

### Transition phrases (Halbert)
Short, conversational phrases that bridge paragraphs and maintain momentum:
- "Onward." / "Well, now." / "And so." / "Here's the thing." / "Listen."
- "But here's where it gets interesting."
- "Before I tell you how, let me first..."
- "Now, you might be wondering..."
- "If you're still with me..."

Place these between paragraphs or sections. They prevent the cliff-reader from bouncing.

### Parenthetical asides (Halbert)
Parentheses give visual eye-relief. Use them to tuck in side notes, clarifications, personal touches. They break up dense text and create rhythm.

Example: "I learned this the hard way (after losing $14K on a campaign that looked great in preview) and I don't want you to make the same mistake."

### Short paragraphs, short sentences
- 1-3 sentences per paragraph in online copy.
- Mix short (3-8 words) and medium (15-20 words) sentences.
- Avoid long (25+ word) sentences in body copy. Save them for single-point emphasis.

### Sentence-per-line formatting (for email)
In emails especially, put every complete thought on its own line — even if it's a sentence fragment. White space pulls the reader down the page.

Example:
> This is a complete sentence.
>
> So is this.
>
> And this.

vs. the dense block alternative which pushes the reader away.

## Questions and Answers (Conversational Copy)

Ask a question. Answer it yourself. This mimics how humans actually think through problems.

### Example
> So why doesn't glucosamine work for every dog?
>
> Simple: most brands use a form that can't cross the intestinal barrier. Your dog eats the pill, but the glucosamine molecule is too big for his body to absorb. Out it goes — expensive pee.
>
> Our form uses a micronized version that solves this.

This pattern is powerful because it simulates conversation. The reader feels like they're in dialogue, not being lectured.

## Voice Rules

### Plain words over fancy words
- "Get" not "procure"
- "Buy" not "purchase" (in most contexts)
- "Help" not "facilitate"
- "Use" not "utilize"
- "Show" not "demonstrate" (in everyday copy — exception below)

### Strong verbs where emphasis is needed
- "Annihilates butt pimples" > "Helps with butt pimples"
- "Discover" > "Learn"
- "Transform" > "Change" (sparingly — overuse blunts it)

### Cut the verb "to be" when possible
- "Is" / "are" / "was" / "were" are weak.
- Rewrite with active, specific verbs.

### Read it aloud
Stumble spots = rewrite spots. If your mouth can't smoothly say it, the reader's eye won't smoothly read it.

### The "you-ratio"
Count "you" / "your" vs. "I" / "me" / "we" / "our". Aim for 3:1 "you" in persuasive copy. Exception: when establishing credibility / backstory, you is temporarily low. Return to "you" density as soon as possible.

## Proof Elements (Woven Throughout)

Proof should be distributed across the copy, not blocked in one "proof section". Types of proof (Schwab):

1. **Construction proof** — how the product is made, ingredients, mechanism
2. **Performance proof** — what it does / has done — results, numbers
3. **Testimony proof** — customer testimonials, expert endorsements, reviews
4. **Test proof** — scientific studies, AB tests, keyed returns, awards

### Specific-proof tactics
- Real names of people quoted
- Specific dates, numbers, dollar amounts
- Photos of results (before/after)
- Screenshots of real reviews (not curated text versions)
- Video testimonials with pull-quote captions (so readers get proof even if they don't play the video)
- Third-party mentions / press quotes
- Certifications, awards, credentials
- Before/after data with methodology

### Weaving
When making a claim, drop a piece of proof within 1-2 sentences. Don't batch all claims, then all proof. Example:

❌ "Our supplement is the best. We use only the highest-quality ingredients. We have thousands of happy customers. [Then 3 paragraphs later:] Here are testimonials..."

✅ "Our supplement is [X benefit] — because we use a micronized form of glucosamine that's 7× more bioavailable than what's in most pet stores. Karen S. from Ohio saw her 12-year-old Labrador back to walking stairs within 3 weeks. [Photo]."

## Picture Words (Halbert)

Every abstract claim must have a concrete picture behind it. If the reader can't see it in their mind, it doesn't register.

### Examples
❌ "Wealth"
✅ "A vacation home in Aspen / a Tesla in the driveway / money your kids will never run out of"

❌ "Freedom"
✅ "Waking up without an alarm. Morning coffee on the porch. Deciding in real-time whether you want to work today."

❌ "Better sleep"
✅ "Falling asleep within 5 minutes of your head hitting the pillow. Sleeping through the whole night. Waking up at 6:30 AM feeling like a kid."

Rule: whenever you write an abstract noun (success, freedom, wealth, peace, comfort), immediately follow with a concrete picture.

## Close / CTA Mechanics

The close is where conversion happens. It is NOT the place for elegant writing. It's the place for precision.

### What every close needs

1. **State the offer clearly** — what they get, what they pay
2. **Specific CTA** — "Click here to get started" / "Buy now" / "Add to cart"
3. **Urgency or reason-why** — deadline, limited stock, price hike coming, bonus expiring
4. **Guarantee** — removes perceived risk
5. **P.S.** (for long-form) — restate key benefit or deadline; P.S. is often read first by skimmers

### Specific tactics

- **Button copy:** "Buy now" / "Get started" / "Claim your spot" — test variants. "Yes, I want the discount" sometimes beats "Buy now" because it's the reader saying yes to themselves.
- **Pre-addressed envelopes / one-click checkouts:** remove every friction.
- **Specific steps:** "Click the button, enter your email, confirm the order." Numbered lists beat vague "just sign up".
- **Deadline in specific terms:** "Ends Friday at 11:59 PM Eastern" > "limited time only".
- **Reason-why for the deadline:** "Because we're only running this promotion while inventory lasts" > bare deadline.

### What to avoid

- Bait-and-switch tactics (fake urgency, fake discounts). Destroys trust long-term.
- Multiple CTAs in one close. Pick ONE action; make it effortless.
- Burying the price. If price is a concern, address it head-on with a reason-why. If it isn't, state it confidently.
