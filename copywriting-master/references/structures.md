# Structural Patterns for Copy

The six structures that cover 99% of direct-response copy. Use the right structure for the format; every structure is a *descriptive* guideline (what the parts DO), not a *prescriptive* template (specific words to use). The principles underneath matter more than the acronym.

---

## RIOA — Rule of One (Copy That)

The checklist to complete BEFORE writing anything. Not a writing structure; a planning tool.

- **R**eader: ONE person being written to
- **I**dea: ONE sales argument / angle / central promise
- **O**ffer: ONE deal / proposition / objective
- **A**ction: ONE next step required

### Example (Copy That's mega-course UGMS page)
- R: working copywriter or marketer who can afford investment, wants more income + freedom
- I: becoming an "everything marketer" beats specializing in one copy discipline
- O: $199 for UGMS course (going up to $399 in 2 weeks) + free bonuses + lifetime access
- A: click the button, check out at $199

If any of R/I/O/A is fuzzy, you aren't ready to write.

## HBC — Hook, Body, Close (Copy That)

The universal copy structure. Scales from a 1-line search ad to a 100-page sales letter.

- **Hook**: grab attention. Stop the right reader, get them into the second sentence.
- **Body**: channel attention toward action. Overcome objections. Build belief.
- **Close**: give the reader the specific way to take action.

### Worked example — search ad (Hook/Body/Close in 10 words)
"**86% off noise-cancelling headphones** — Sony, Bose, Beats — **today only**"
- Hook: the disruptive offer itself (86% off is unusual)
- Body: three brand names overcome the "cheap = bad quality" objection
- Close: "today only" = urgency + implicit CTA

### Worked example — long-form sales letter
- Hook: headline (2-3 seconds to stop the right reader)
- Body: 10-50 pages of:
  - Lead (emotional hook, promise, story, secret, whatever matches awareness)
  - Credibility establishment
  - Big idea reveal
  - Proof of mechanism
  - Objection handling (OCPB cycles)
  - Offer construction
  - Bonuses
- Close: order form, guarantee, urgency, P.S. emphasizing key point

## OCPB — Objection, Claim, Proof, Benefit (Copy That / Sean McIntyre)

The body-copy structure for overcoming a single objection. Cycle OCPB for each major objection the reader has.

- **O**bjection: state the objection (often as a question)
- **C**laim: make a counter-claim (often reframe or sell-against-alternatives)
- **P**roof: back up the claim with specific evidence (numbers, dates, examples, expert quotes, track record)
- **B**enefit: tie back to what the reader stands to gain

For skeptical audiences or high-stakes decisions: use **OCPPPB** (multi-proof) — stack 3 proofs before the benefit reveal.

### Worked example (Sean's financial webinar, 2019)
- O: "What would you say to someone watching who isn't sure about getting into the markets right now?"
- C: "Take the financial media — you'll never make money listening to them. They sell fear and what has already happened."
- P1 (logic): mainstream media focuses on what's already big and known.
- P2 (specific example): May 6, 2010 flash crash; NYT reported investors fleeing; Wall Street economist predicted another drop.
- P3 (track record): "But I staked my reputation when I put down in print on July 6, 2010 that the S&P 500 should go back to all-time highs."
- B: "Had you bought then, every $1 would have become $3."

### When NOT to use OCPB
- Low-stakes purchases (commodities)
- Readers who are Most-aware (they don't need objection-handling — they need the offer)
- Short-form ads (no room)

## DIC — Disrupt, Intrigue, Click (Copy That lift-email convention)

The structure for SHORT emails whose only job is to get the click. Use when the destination page does the actual selling (long-form sales letter, VSL, product page with copy).

- **D**isrupt: pattern-interrupt the inbox scroller. Something surprising, novel, or highly specific.
- **I**ntrigue: build fascination with specifics. Tease WITHOUT revealing. The curiosity gap = click energy.
- **C**lick: direct ask + link.

### Why keep it short
If you recap the sales argument in the email, you sap the persuasive power of the destination page. The reader has mentally "already heard it" when they arrive, even if the email gave a simplified version. The destination needs to hit them fresh.

### Worked example (Todd Brown's lift-email style)
> Kicking off a crazy Black Friday deal for you tonight.
>
> This gives you 85% off a hand-selected collection of 17 private market trainings.
>
> And FYI, none of these trainings are available for purchase separately anywhere. They were created for my top-tier clients.
>
> But right now, you can access them all for pennies on the dollar.
>
> [LINK]

Disrupt = "crazy Black Friday deal tonight". Intrigue = "17 private market trainings, top-tier only, pennies on the dollar". Click = direct.

### DIC subsumes AIDA, PAS, Four P's, etc.
DIC is *descriptive* — tells you what EFFECT each section should have. AIDA / PAS / 4P's are *prescriptive* — tell you what to say. Descriptive > prescriptive because it preserves your freedom to find what works.

## AIDA — Attention, Interest, Desire, Action (Classic; taught by Halbert)

The oldest structure still in use. Same purpose as DIC but with more granularity.

- **A**ttention: stop the reader. Halbert's methods: attach a dollar bill, a coin, a baggie of sand, a feather, a lottery ticket — anything that survives the 3-second-scan.
- **I**nterest: feed them interesting facts, specifics, story elements. Halbert: "cubic tons of sand pumped to build the Hawaiian shore."
- **D**esire: paint benefits in real life. Halbert: "new car, vacations, attracting mate, leisure, respect."
- **A**ction: tell them EXACTLY what to do. Specific steps. Halbert spends up to 25% of an ad on the close.

Use AIDA as a mental map, not a template. Violate the order when it serves the reader (e.g., start with desire for a Most-aware audience).

## 5-Part Sales Letter (Masterson — *Architecture of Persuasion*)

Direct-mail architecture. Scales to VSLs, long-form sales pages, anywhere the piece does the whole sale itself.

1. **Envelope** (or email subject line / ad creative / thumbnail): must get opened. Halbert's A-pile rule applies.
2. **Headline**: stop the right reader. Caples: a headline change alone can 5-10× returns.
3. **Lead**: first 400-800 words (or first 10% of long-form). Does 80% of the emotional work. See `lead-types.md` for the 6 types by awareness.
4. **Argument**: the long middle. Credibility, proof, mechanism, case studies, objection-handling, differentiation. OCPB cycles here. 3 Dimensions of Body Copy (Schwartz): alter their desires, their identifications, their beliefs.
5. **Close**: offer reveal, price, bonuses, guarantee, urgency, CTA, P.S. P.S. is often read first — put a key benefit or deadline there.

### What order to write
Many pro copywriters write out of order:
1. Lead / headline (establishes tone)
2. Close (forces you to know what you're actually selling)
3. Middle (argument) fills in between

Why: lead + close frame the piece. Middle is fungible; lead and close aren't.

## 16-Word Sales Letter — One Belief + 10 Questions (Evaldo Albuquerque)

Scalable structure for long-form VSLs. Particularly effective for financial / info / health.

- **One Belief**: the single conviction you want the reader to adopt by the end. (Often: "There is a hidden [mechanism/force/secret] that affects [their life/finances/health]; my [product] is the way to harness/avoid it.")
- **10 Questions**: the logical chain from their current state to believing the One Belief. Each question builds on the previous.

### Generic template structure
1. What is the MECHANISM behind the thing happening to them?
2. WHY should they care?
3. WHO is causing it / controlling it?
4. WHAT PROOF exists it's real?
5. WHY hasn't anyone told them?
6. WHAT is their situation right now (unaware)?
7. WHAT are the consequences of not acting?
8. WHAT IS the solution?
9. HOW specifically does it work?
10. WHAT should they do next?

Not every question needs equal weight. Balance based on awareness stage.

## Structure selection flowchart

```
Is the reader going to a separate sales page that does the selling?
├── YES → DIC (lift email)
└── NO → The copy must sell itself
    │
    Is it short (< 100 words)?
    ├── YES → HBC condensed (one hook line + 2-3 body lines + close)
    └── NO → Does it have to persuade a sophisticated/skeptical audience?
        ├── YES & long-form → 5-Part Sales Letter with OCPB cycles in body
        ├── YES & info/financial/health → 16-Word Sales Letter framework
        └── NO → HBC with light body
```

---

## Universal rule

Whatever structure you use: the HOOK must grab, the BODY must persuade, the CLOSE must instruct. These three functions must happen — even if they collapse into a single sentence. A piece of copy with no hook, no persuasion, and no ask is a piece of writing, not copy.
